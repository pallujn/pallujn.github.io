Why Your Dog Pulls on the Leash (and How to Teach Loose-Leash Walking)

By Jonathan Pereira · Updated November 20, 2026

If you want to know how to stop a dog pulling on the leash, don’t start by pulling back. A tight leash often turns the walk into a tug-of-war — and your dog’s chest will usually win.

Pulling is not a character flaw. It is a behavior that has been paying off: the sidewalk is interesting, and leaning in has been the way to get there. You can teach a different deal: the walk moves when the leash is loose.

Why pulling keeps happening

From your dog’s point of view, the world is a buffet of smells, movement, and other animals. Walking at a human pace, by a human hip, is a learned skill — not a default setting.

Pulling is also mechanically sensible. If leaning into the collar or harness has historically made the walk continue toward the interesting thing, your dog will lean again. The tighter you pull back, the more some dogs brace and drive forward. You get a loop: tension, more tension, a less pleasant walk for both of you.

Excitement and frustration can stack on top of that. A dog who has been waiting all day, or who is already buzzing at the door, is a harder student. A short settle or a few sniffing minutes in the yard before you start can lower the volume. That is a different skill from “more miles,” and it is one we cover in Why a Tired Dog Still Won’t Settle.

What not to do

Don’t make the walk a contest of strength. Yanking, “leash pops,” or prong or shock tools are not part of our plan. They can add pain or fear, and they still do not teach your dog what does make the walk go.

Don’t only correct the tight leash and never pay the loose one. If slack never earns anything, your dog has little reason to offer it.

Don’t start the lesson on the busiest street you know. You would not teach a new piano student at a concert.

Don’t skip the first thirty seconds at the door. If the exit is a explosion of jumping and a sprint to the end of the leash, the walk has already started on a tight note. Wait for four paws and a quieter body, then step out. (The jumping article and the checklist’s “Calm Hello” use the same idea.)

Gear is management, not the whole plan

Equipment can make the walk safer and more comfortable while you teach.

A front-clip harness (a leash that attaches on the chest) can redirect some of the forward pull and is a common management choice for dogs who lunge into a neck collar. A well-fitted harness should not rub, ride up into the throat, or leave your dog able to slip out. If you are unsure about fit, ask a trainer or a knowledgeable store associate to check it on your dog.

For a closer look at specific models, see our guide to no-pull harnesses. Treat gear as a helper, not a substitute for the reset below. Head collars are another tool some owners use with slow, careful introduction; they are not a first recommendation in this article because they require a patient fitting process and are not comfortable for every dog.

Avoid stacking harsher hardware on top of a frustrating walk. If your dog coughs, wheezes, or paws at the equipment, stop and change the setup.

The leash reset

This is the “pay for slack” deal, the same idea as Day 3 of the free checklist.

1. Start with a loose leash in a low-distraction place. A quiet driveway, an empty parking lot, or a hallway can be enough for the first sessions. Have small, soft treats you can deliver quickly.

2. The moment the leash goes slack beside you, mark and pay. You can also pay a glance toward you. You are buying the position you want, not waiting for a perfect heel.

3. If the leash tightens, stop being interesting. Become a tree: stop walking. Wait. When the leash bends again, mark, then move forward. Alternatively, calmly change direction so that catching up to you is the way to get to go somewhere.

4. Reward the catch-up. The second they close the gap and the leash softens, pay at your side. Then walk on.

5. Keep the session short. Several two- or three-minute practices will teach more than one long, frustrating circuit around the block.

Talk less, pay more. A stream of “easy, easy, no, heel” becomes background noise. The tight-or-slack consequence, plus food at your side, is clearer.

How to practice

Build difficulty the way you would build any skill:

  • Master slack in the quiet place.
  • Add slow movement.
  • Add a slightly more interesting route (a familiar side street).
  • Then add harder moments — another dog at a distance, a scent-rich verge — and go back a step if the leash goes tight and stays tight.

Let your dog sniff on a loose leash as part of the walk, not as a prize they can only reach by dragging you. Sniffing is a real need. You can say “go sniff” when the leash is already soft, then call them on and pay when they return to your side.

If your dog is over-aroused before you leave, do a one-minute mat settle or scatter a handful of kibble in the grass first. A buzzing brain makes a tight leash more likely.

When to get extra help

If pulling comes with hard lunging, snapping, or a history of bites, this article is not the right tool. Work with a qualified professional and, if needed, a veterinarian.

If you have practiced the reset in easy places for a couple of weeks and cannot get any slack, a skilled local trainer can watch your timing and your dog’s body language in person. That is not a failure. It is a good use of help.

FAQ

How long will this take?

There is no honest universal timeline. Dogs who have been paid for pulling for years will need more easy repetitions than a young puppy. Measure progress by “more slack on the quiet street,” not by a calendar promise.

Should I use a retractable leash while I teach this?

We do not recommend retractable leashes for this lesson. They often keep a light, constant tension and make the “slack vs. tight” picture harder to read. A regular leash of a length you can manage is clearer.

Is a front-clip harness “cheating”?

No. Management that keeps your dog more comfortable while you pay for slack is fair. Just keep teaching the skill, or the habit will return when the gear changes.

What if my dog plants and won’t walk?

Don’t drag. Wait, take a step the other way, or toss a treat slightly forward to restart movement, then pay walking with you. A sudden freeze can also be worry or pain — if it is new, check with your vet.
